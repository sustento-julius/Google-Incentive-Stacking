3 Mins Local

The fastest way to understand what actually happened at LA City Hall.

🏛️ Reads city council meetings 🎤 Finds the best public comment 📰 Writes a 3-minute newsletter

💡 What is this thing?

Imagine a really sharp friend who actually sits through every six-hour LA City Council meeting so you don't have to — then texts you the good parts.

City government is important but painfully boring to follow. Meetings run for hours, the agendas are 1,400-page PDFs, and everything is written in confusing government-speak.

3 Mins Local is a robot that does the boring part. It grabs the meeting, reads the whole transcript, figures out what mattered, picks the most interesting thing a regular person said at the podium, translates the jargon into plain English, and writes a short, funny, easy-to-read newsletter — about a 3-minute read.

📨 Think of it as "Morning Brew for city government" — or The Daily Show meets your local news. You finish each issue knowing what happened, why it matters, and you skipped the six hours of meetings.

⚙️ How it works, step by step

The app is an assembly line. Raw meeting goes in one end; a finished newsletter comes out the other. Here are the stations:

1

📅 Find the meetings

It checks the LA City Clerk's online calendar for recent council and committee meetings. (If the city's website blocks it, the app uses built-in sample meetings so it always works.)

2

📝 Get the transcript

The city often posts a written transcript ("CART"). If it does, the app reads that. If there's only a video, the app can listen to the audio and type it out automatically (using a tool called Whisper). A transcript is just the meeting, written down.

3

🙋 Figure out who's talking

It sorts every line by speaker: councilmember, city staff, the clerk, or a member of the public. This is how it knows which parts are real public testimony.

4

🏆 Pick the best public comment

Every public comment gets a score on four things: is it newsworthy, emotional, quotable/viral, and does it tie to a real vote? The highest score becomes the "Public Comment of the Day."

5

🔤 Translate the government-speak

It decodes jargon into plain English. The classic example:
"mobility corridor optimization" → "we're turning a car lane into a bus lane."

6

📰 Write the newsletter

Finally it assembles everything into the finished issue — subject line, hook, bullets, big story, the public comment, "LA Vibes," numbers, and what's next — kept to about 500–700 words (a 3-minute read). It saves the result as a file you can read or email.

🧠 Two "brains" — and why there's a Google key

The writing in step 6 can be done two ways. The app picks automatically.

Always works · free

🤖 The built-in writer (templates)

A set of smart fill-in-the-blank rules that I wrote into the app. It always produces a complete, correct newsletter with zero cost and no internet. The phrasing is a little more repetitive issue-to-issue.

Optional · needs a Google key

✨ The AI writer (Google Gemini)

When you add a Google API key, the app asks Google's AI to write fresh, witty, human-sounding prose for each issue. Same structure — better, less repetitive writing.

"Why can't you just write it?" — I did! The rules for how the newsletter is built are my writing. But the app is meant to run on its own every day, on meetings nobody has read yet. At that moment I'm not there to write — so it needs a writer on call. The built-in templates are that writer for free; Google's AI is an upgraded writer you can plug in. You do not need the Google key for the app to work.

🎛️ What you can change

You can adjust a lot without being a coder. Each card says how hard it is and which file holds the setting.

Easy

The personality / voice

Make it funnier, more serious, more sarcastic, more neighborly. The "voice instructions" are written in plain English you can edit.

file: generation/prompts.py
Easy

Which topics count as "big news"

Add or remove the subjects the app cares about — e.g. add "schools" or "parks" so comments about them rank higher.

file: config.py → TOPIC_KEYWORDS
Easy

The jargon dictionary

Add your own "government-speak → plain English" translations. This is just a list of phrase pairs.

file: config.py → GOVERNMENT_PHRASEBOOK
Easy

Newsletter length

Want a 2-minute read or a 5-minute one? Change the target word count numbers.

file: config.py → TARGET_WORDS…
Medium

How comments are scored

Decide whether "emotional" moments or "ties to a real vote" matter more, by changing the weighting numbers.

file: processing/ranker.py → WEIGHTS
Medium

Turn the AI writer on/off

Add a Google key to a file named .env to switch on the AI writer; remove it to go back to the free built-in writer.

file: .env → GEMINI_API_KEY

🔨 How it was built

It's written in Python, a popular and readable programming language. The code is organized like the assembly line above — each station is its own small file in a folder called src/three_mins_local/:

  • sources/ — gets the meeting list and the transcript
  • processing/ — sorts speakers, finds & scores public comments, analyzes the agenda
  • generation/ — writes the actual newsletter (templates or AI)
  • fixtures/ — the built-in sample meetings, so it always runs
  • tests/ — 28 automatic checks that confirm each part works

A key design idea: nothing ever fully breaks. If the city website is down, it uses samples. If there's no transcript, it can transcribe the video. If there's no AI key, it uses the built-in writer. The app always produces a finished newsletter.

❓ Quick questions

Do I have to pay for anything?

No. The app runs free using the built-in writer and sample data. The optional Google AI key may have a free tier, and heavy use can cost money — but it's entirely optional.

Is it making things up?

It's built to stay factual — it only writes from the transcript and agenda it was given, and it's specifically told not to invent votes, names, or quotes. The AI writer is also instructed never to mock ordinary residents — humor targets bureaucracy, not people.

Can it cover cities other than LA?

Yes, with some setup. The "find the meetings" and transcript steps are LA-specific today, but the scoring, translation, and newsletter parts would work anywhere you can feed in a transcript.

How do I actually run it?

A coder (or me) runs one command — python -m three_mins_local run --print — and it prints the newsletter and saves it as a file. The full instructions are in the project's README.
